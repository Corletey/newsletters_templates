A Defining Moment for African Climate Action

Africa is home to the world’s youngest population — and some of its most severe climate impacts. Across the continent, young people are not waiting for solutions. They are building them: climate-smart farms, renewable energy ventures, digital platforms connecting farmers to markets, and community-led adaptation initiatives. Yet too often, these innovations remain underfunded, disconnected from institutions, and unable to scale.

The Africa Climate Collaborative 2026 Convening, held in Nairobi, Kenya from 20–24 April, was designed to change that.

The Africa Climate Collaborative is one of the Mastercard Foundation’s seven Collaboratives driving systems change in critical sectors for Africa’s socio-economic development. The Collaborative brings together four leading universities — the University of Cambridge, the University of Cape Town, the University of Ghana, and Makerere University — along with key institutional partners, including the Association of African Universities (AAU), which serves as the Collaborative’s backbone.

Bringing together researchers, youth leaders, entrepreneurs, and decision-makers from partner institutions, the convening asked a harder question: What does it actually take to move from ambition to action?

The 2026 Convening was designed to catalyse this transition, building from strategic co-creation to robust operationalisation. The gathering served as the pivotal platform to ratify core frameworks, accelerate joint initiatives, and solidify the internal architecture required for sustained impact. It was a strategic working session dedicated to aligning efforts, finalising foundational tools, and strengthening the partnerships at the heart of the Collaborative’s mission. Over five days, participants moved beyond conversation to tackle the systems, partnerships, and investments needed to turn African-led climate solutions into lasting impact.

 

Opening sessions, Africa Climate Collaborative 2026 Convening — Nairobi, Kenya

What We Heard: Young Africans Are Not Waiting

Young people are not merely participants in climate programmes — they are driving them. This message resonated throughout the convening, particularly during a dedicated youth session that brought together innovators, entrepreneurs, and programme representatives from across the continent.

Participants shared what is already working. In their communities, young people are leading climate-smart agriculture and agroforestry initiatives, building digital platforms that connect farmers to markets, establishing maritime cooperatives that promote sustainable livelihoods, and designing leadership programmes that strengthen the capacity of emerging climate innovators. These are not pilot projects. They are proofs of concept — demonstrations that when young people are trusted and supported, they deliver.

But the session also surfaced what is not working.

“Many of us are over-mentored and under-capitalised.”

— Youth Session Participant, ACC 2026 Convening, Nairobi

 

Training programmes and pitch competitions are abundant. What young innovators need is different: patient capital, long-term mentorship, access to technology, and pathways to influence policy decisions that affect their futures.

Participants named the barriers plainly. Limited access to funding remains the most persistent obstacle. Mentorship networks are often fragmented or inaccessible. Policy spaces remain closed to young people, even when policies directly affect their livelihoods. And too often, youth engagement is consultative rather than substantive — a single session rather than a seat at the table.

What emerged was a clear demand: young people want to move beyond being consulted. They want to co-design, govern, and lead. They want structured mechanisms that allow them to contribute consistently to programme design, implementation, and decision-making — not just at events, but across the life of every initiative that claims to serve them.

“Africa does not lack talents, ideas or ambition. What needs strengthening are the systems that help those ideas and talents grow.”

— Delta40 Site Visit, Nairobi

Africa Does Not Lack Talents, Ideas or Ambition

This message landed differently outside the conference room. During a site visit to Delta40, a venture studio based in Nairobi, Kenya, participants saw firsthand what it takes to build climate ventures in Africa. Discussions with founders and ecosystem builders revealed a sobering reality: innovation is not the problem. Execution and scale are.

The visit focused on what entrepreneurs call the “valley of death” — the stage where promising ventures struggle to grow due to limited financing, weak infrastructure, and insufficient operational support. This challenge is particularly acute in sectors like clean energy, climate technology, and sustainable food systems, where entrepreneurs often require significant capital and long-term backing to scale their solutions.

Delta40 offers a different model. Rather than providing short-term acceleration, the venture studio works closely with entrepreneurs over extended periods, offering embedded support in finance, operations, and commercialisation. This hands-on approach helps ventures navigate the complex challenges of growth while building the capacity needed for long-term sustainability.

 

Delta40 site visit, Nairobi — participants engaged directly with founders and ecosystem builders

The visit also highlighted a persistent gap between research and commercialisation within African universities. Many researchers develop innovative solutions with significant potential — but lack the structures, partnerships, and support needed to bring those ideas to market.

“If strong institutions existed to support commercialisation, more researchers would be entrepreneurs and research findings won’t only stay in labs.”

Convening participant

“I didn’t set out to build an organisation. I set out to solve a problem. But the systems weren’t there. So, I had to build them myself.”

Delta40 founder

The site visit reinforced a central message of the convening: Africa has no shortage of talent, ambition, or innovative ideas. What is needed are stronger systems that support execution, provide long-term backing for entrepreneurs, and connect research with real-world application.

Green Jobs Project • University of Cambridge • Two-Year Research Initiative

Are Universities Preparing Students for Today’s Green Jobs — or the Future Green Economy?

This question shaped a session on the Green Jobs Project, a two-year research initiative led by the University of Cambridge in collaboration with partner universities and youth organisations across Africa.

The research was distinguished by its approach: young people were not simply research participants but active partners in shaping questions, collecting data, analysing findings, and sharing results. This ensured that the experiences and perspectives of young people remained central throughout.

The findings confirmed both opportunity and challenge. Africa’s green economy holds vast potential across renewable energy, sustainable agriculture, waste management, ecosystem restoration, and environmental services. But significant barriers continue to limit youth participation.

During the panel discussion, it was emphasised that low awareness of green careers remains a persistent challenge. Many young people are unfamiliar with the opportunities emerging in green industries. Social perceptions also influence career choices — sectors like agriculture and waste management are often viewed as less desirable despite their growing importance. Gender inequalities, limited access to resources, and skills gaps further restrict opportunities for many young people.

 

“If we keep teaching students the way we were taught, we will prepare them for a world that no longer exists.”

— Panel discussant, Green Jobs Session, ACC 2026 Convening

The discussion called for greater investment in skills development, entrepreneurship support, and climate-focused financing mechanisms. Panellists emphasised the need for education and training systems better aligned with labour market demands, as well as policies that encourage innovation and green enterprise development.

A key message emerged: universities must move beyond traditional academic models and equip students with practical, entrepreneurial, and interdisciplinary skills. Stronger partnerships between universities, industry, governments, and communities are essential for ensuring that graduates are prepared to respond to evolving workforce needs. Indigenous knowledge systems, too, must be recognised as valuable contributors to environmental stewardship and climate solutions.

Research Alone Is Not Enough: Universities Must Become Ecosystem Builders

A high-level panel discussion on climate innovation pushed this thinking further. Bringing together representatives from academia, industry, development organisations, and youth entrepreneurship, the session explored how higher education institutions can strengthen climate innovation, support entrepreneurship, and prepare young people to lead Africa’s green transition.

The message was clear: universities cannot afford to remain ivory towers. They must become convenors — spaces where researchers, policymakers, industry leaders, entrepreneurs, and communities come together to co-create solutions.

Prof. Olusola Oyewole

Secretary-General, Association of African Universities

  

Laura Kasuni Wathe

Youth entrepreneur

“Universities cannot do this alone. We need industry, we need government, we need communities, and we need young people at the table.”

Prof. Olusola Oyewole, Secretary-General, AAU

“Many graduates face challenges when transitioning from education to enterprise development. We need greater emphasis on mentorship, systems thinking, and community engagement.”

Laura Kasuni Wathe, entrepreneur

The discussion explored the concept of “living laboratories” — universities working directly with communities, entrepreneurs, and other stakeholders to develop and test solutions in real-world settings. Such approaches, participants agreed, ensure that innovations are grounded in local realities and respond to the needs of the communities they are intended to serve.

Inclusion was another central theme. Panelists underscored the importance of ensuring that women, persons with disabilities, displaced populations, and young people from underserved communities are meaningfully represented within climate innovation ecosystems. Building resilient and sustainable solutions requires the active participation of those most affected by climate-related challenges.

Ultimately, the session reinforced a key message of the convening: addressing climate challenges requires more than research alone. It demands collaboration, inclusion, and a commitment to transforming ideas into practical solutions that create lasting impact for communities across Africa.

A Collective Voice: Strengthening Communications Across the Collaborative

Effective climate action depends not only on innovative solutions but also on the ability to communicate those solutions to the people who need them most. Recognising this, communications leads and programme representatives from partner institutions convened a landmark workshop in Nairobi — the Collaborative’s first in-person Communications and Branding engagement.

The session brought together representatives from the University of Cambridge, the University of Cape Town, the University of Ghana, Makerere University, the Association of African Universities, and the Mastercard Foundation.

Thuli Montana — Lead, Africa Climate Collaborative

“The Africa Climate Collaborative was established to support higher education institutions in driving climate-positive growth through research, innovation, skills development and youth empowerment. But if we don’t tell the story — if we don’t show what is working, who is leading, what we are learning and what is changing — the impact remains invisible.”

 

Communications leads and programme representatives from partner institutions at the Nairobi workshop

Participants acknowledged a persistent challenge: valuable research, innovation, and community impact often remain underrepresented due to fragmented communication efforts. By strengthening collaboration and adopting a shared storytelling framework, the institutions aim to increase the reach and influence of their collective work.

Each institution highlighted its priorities — including strengthening project visibility through cohesive branding and sharing impact stories from researchers and fellows; ensuring that climate opportunities reach underserved young people; and strengthening systems for measuring and communicating impact.

“If you are findable, you are fundable.”

ACC Communications and Branding Workshop, Nairobi

The workshop reinforced a critical understanding: communication is not a supporting function. It is a strategic tool for advancing climate action. By sharing stories of innovation, research, entrepreneurship, and community impact, universities can shape public discourse, influence policy, attract partnerships, and inspire broader participation in climate solutions.

Participants left with a clearer roadmap for implementing the Africa Climate Collaborative’s communication strategy and ensuring greater consistency in branding, storytelling, and audience engagement. By speaking with one collective voice, the Collaborative is laying the foundation for greater visibility, collaboration, and influence in the years ahead.

What We Learned

The Nairobi convening revealed something important about the state of climate action in Africa: the solutions exist. The people driving them are capable, determined, and ready. What is missing are the systems — the financing, the mentorship, the policy support, the institutional connections — that allow those solutions to grow. Across five days of presentations, panel discussions, site visits, and working sessions, several foundational insights crystallised:

01 — Climate Is a Development Challenge

Climate change in Africa spans employment, food security, education, fiscal stability, social cohesion, and political resilience. Addressing it requires structural responses integrating economic transformation, social inclusion, and institutional reform. “We cannot treat climate as separate from livelihoods. For Africa, they are the same conversation.”

02 — Youth Agency Strengthens Outcomes

Where young people are positioned as co-creators, implementers, entrepreneurs, and knowledge producers — rather than passive beneficiaries — solutions are more relevant, durable, and scalable. NEET youth and informal-sector innovators remain frequently underrepresented in institutional programmes.

03 — Systems, Not Just Ideas

Africa does not lack ideas. What it lacks are coordinated, adaptive systems that translate assets into durable, equitable impact at scale. Demand for climate-related skills and entrepreneurship far exceeds institutional capacity.

04 — Universities as Ecosystem Leaders

Universities must become convenors where researchers, policymakers, industry leaders, entrepreneurs, and communities co-create solutions. This requires institutional courage: reforming incentives, redistributing power, and investing in coordination capacity.

05 — Finance: The Missing Middle

Many ventures struggle to grow due to limited financing, weak infrastructure, and insufficient operational support. Access to patient capital, technical expertise, and long-term strategic support remains scarce. The challenge is not the volume of funding, but the availability of appropriate financial instruments.

06 — Governance, Evidence & Communications

A Programme Charter defines purpose. A Theory of Change and MEL framework generates evidence. Communications amplify impact and build credibility. None functions effectively in isolation. These are mutually reinforcing systems.

07 — Equity Must Be Structural

Inclusion means not just access, but power and value capture — who controls finance, research priorities, enterprise ownership, and decision-making. “Inclusion as access is not enough. We need inclusion as ownership.”

08 — Coordination Is as Critical as Financing

Fragmentation and unclear ownership repeatedly undermine promising initiatives. Backbone functions played by the AAU are essential. The ACC’s alignment with the AU’s CESA 2026–2035 framework provides a pathway for scaling models beyond pilots to continental systems.

09 — Prepare for Global Policy Engagement

COP31 and COP32 represent critical opportunities for African universities and youth leaders to shape continental climate narratives. But meaningful participation requires intentional preparation, realistic expectations, and clear ownership of coordination and messaging. The Collaborative must arrive not as observers, but as contributors with evidence-backed positions.

What Comes Next

The conversations in Nairobi were not an endpoint. They were a beginning — the moment when the Collaborative shifted from alignment to activation. Commitments made in that room are already being followed up.

Venture studios like Delta40 have committed to deepening their engagement with university-based innovators, bridging the gap between research, talent, and market execution. Industry partners have pledged to strengthen pathways between graduates and green employment opportunities. The Collaborative is now moving decisively into its next phase of implementation.

Partner institutions will continue advancing research, innovation, climate entrepreneurship, skills development, and youth-focused programmes across the continent. Upcoming activities will include the rollout of climate-related training programmes, support for green enterprise development, continued research and fellowship activities, expansion of innovation partnerships, and enhanced engagement with policymakers and communities.

Building on the momentum generated in Nairobi, the Collaborative remains committed to translating ambition into action and strengthening pathways for young Africans to contribute meaningfully to climate resilience and sustainable development.
